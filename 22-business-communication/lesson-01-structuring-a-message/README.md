# Lesson 1: Structuring a Written Message

## Objective

Write emails, reports, and short documents that get read and acted on — using a clear, front-loaded structure instead of a stream-of-consciousness narrative the reader has to dig through to find the point.

## Prerequisites

None.

## Learn

**Lead with the point, not the journey to it.** Most weak business writing narrates in chronological order — background, then context, then eventually the actual ask or conclusion. A reader (especially a busy one skimming) needs the point first: what you want, what happened, what decision is needed — then supporting detail for whoever needs it. This is the same "bottom line up front" (BLUF) principle used in military and consulting writing, and it exists because readers stop reading before the end far more often than writers assume.

**One message, one purpose.** An email trying to inform, request a decision, and vent frustration simultaneously usually accomplishes none of the three well — the reader can't tell which parts require action and which are just context. Before writing, decide explicitly: is this informing, requesting, or persuading? Structure follows from that answer.

**Structure for skimming, not just for reading start to finish.** Short paragraphs, one idea per paragraph, and visual structure (a numbered list for sequential items, a short table for comparisons) let a reader extract what they need without reading every sentence — a genuine kindness to a recipient who's triaging dozens of messages, not a stylistic nicety.

**Match formality and length to the actual relationship and stakes.** A message to a professor requesting an exam accommodation needs different formality than a Slack message to a teammate about a bug — over-formal reads as stiff and evasive in a casual context; under-formal reads as careless in a high-stakes one. This is a judgment call, not a fixed rule, and it's worth making deliberately rather than defaulting to one register for everything.

## Attempt

1. Take a real email or message you've sent recently (or plan to send) that buries its point — rewrite it with the actual ask/conclusion in the first sentence, then supporting detail after.

2. Write three versions of the same request (e.g. asking a professor for a deadline extension) at three formality levels: very formal, standard professional, casual — and identify which is actually appropriate for that real context and why.

3. Take a paragraph of dense, run-on prose (your own, or a sample) and restructure it: break it into short paragraphs, extract any sequential or comparative content into a list or table, and confirm the restructured version is faster to skim and extract the key point from.

4. Draft a real, upcoming message you actually need to send (an email to an administration office, a message to a group project teammate) using this lesson's structure — point first, one purpose, skimmable formatting, appropriate register.

## Verify

Show the before/after of your step 1 rewrite side by side, and have someone else (or reread it yourself after a break) time how long it takes to identify the actual point in each version — the rewritten version should be noticeably faster.

## Failure drill

Take your step 4 real message and deliberately bury the actual ask in the third paragraph, behind two paragraphs of context and pleasantries — send this version to yourself or read it aloud, and notice how much attention and patience it demands from a reader before they even learn what you want. Explain why a genuinely busy recipient (a professor with 200 students, an office handling dozens of requests) is disproportionately likely to skim past the ask entirely in this version, compared to the point-first version — and why that's not a hypothetical risk but the direct, likely cost of this specific structural mistake.

## Transfer

Apply this lesson's structure to a message you'll need to send this semester for real — a request to a professor, an administrative inquiry, a message coordinating a group assignment — and use the point-first, one-purpose, skimmable structure deliberately rather than defaulting to habit.

## Done when

You can restructure a buried-point message into a clear one and explain specifically what changed, you've chosen formality deliberately for a real context rather than defaulting to one register, and you've sent (or are ready to send) a real message using this lesson's structure.
