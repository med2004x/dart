# Lesson 2: Professional Email and Correspondence

## Objective

Write emails that follow professional convention — subject lines that convey content, appropriate openings/closings, and correct handling of CC/reply-all — the specific mechanics that signal competence or its absence before the reader even reads the body.

## Prerequisites

Lesson 1 (message structure — this lesson covers the email-specific conventions wrapping that structure).

## Learn

**The subject line is not an afterthought — it's often the only part read before a decision to open (or ignore) the email is made.** A vague subject ("Question," "Hello") forces the recipient to open the email just to find out what it's about, and in a high-volume inbox, often means it gets deprioritized or missed. A specific subject ("Request: extension for [assignment name], due [date]") lets the recipient triage correctly without opening anything.

**Openings and closings signal register, and mismatched register reads as either presumptuous or oddly stiff.** A cold email to an administrator or professor conventionally opens with a greeting using their title ("Dear Professor X" or the local convention equivalent) and closes with a formal sign-off — not because the content demands it, but because the convention itself signals respect for the relationship's actual formality. Skipping this in a first-contact email, even with perfectly reasonable content, can read as careless regardless of the writer's actual intent.

**CC and reply-all: real, not incidental, communication choices.** CC-ing someone signals "you should know about this but I'm not asking you to act." Reply-all in a thread with many recipients should be a deliberate choice, not a default — replying-all with content only relevant to the original sender clutters everyone else's inbox and signals a lack of awareness of who actually needs to see what. Before hitting reply-all, the actual question is: does everyone on this thread need to see my response, or just the original sender?

**Threading and context: don't force the recipient to reconstruct history.** If replying to an old thread about something new, starting a new email with a clear subject is often better than reviving a stale thread whose subject no longer matches the content — a recipient scanning a thread with an unrelated subject line for context they need is a real, avoidable friction point.

## Attempt

1. Write 5 subject lines for 5 different real or plausible scenarios (requesting a meeting, reporting a bug, following up on an unanswered email, asking for feedback, requesting an extension) — each specific enough that the recipient knows exactly what's being asked before opening.

2. Draft a first-contact email to a professor or administrator (a real one you might plausibly need to send) with correct opening/closing convention for your actual academic context, and identify what would signal disrespect or carelessness if omitted.

3. Construct a scenario with 4 email recipients where 2 need to be CC'd (informed, no action needed) and 2 are the actual "To" recipients (action needed) — write the email and justify each placement explicitly.

4. Take a long, multi-person email thread (a real one, or construct one) and identify the exact point where a reply should have started a new thread with a new subject instead of continuing the old one, because the topic had genuinely shifted.

## Verify

Present your 5 subject lines and explain what a recipient could correctly infer from each without opening the email — confirm none require opening to understand the basic ask.

## Failure drill

Take your step 3 CC/To email and deliberately swap the two categories — put action-required content only in the CC line, and put purely informational content in the To line. Reread it from each recipient's perspective and identify exactly where confusion would occur: a CC'd person might now think they need to act (unnecessary work and confusion) while a To recipient might miss that they're actually expected to respond (a real, missed action). Explain why this mismatch is a common, avoidable mistake that costs real time and creates real confusion, not just a stylistic slip.

## Transfer

Find a real email you've sent (or received) that has one of this lesson's specific problems — a vague subject line, wrong CC/To placement, mismatched formality for the relationship — and rewrite it correctly, explaining what specifically was wrong and why the fix matters for that real context.

## Done when

You can write specific, content-conveying subject lines on demand, you've correctly applied formal opening/closing convention to a real first-contact email, and you've correctly reasoned through a real or realistic CC/To decision and can explain the distinction clearly.
